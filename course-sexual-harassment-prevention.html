Course Lessons

Every employee is entitled to a harassment-free, respectful workplace. This opening lesson sets the tone — what the course covers, why it matters, and what a healthy work environment actually looks like.
What does person-to-person sexual harassment actually look like? This lesson covers the key principle that intent does not equal impact — the effect of behavior on someone else is what matters, regardless of what you meant.
A plain-language explanation of quid pro quo harassment — when job benefits, assignments, or advancement are explicitly or implicitly tied to sexual favors or compliance. Real scenarios make this easy to recognize.
Reporting harassment is not just okay — it's protected. This lesson covers how and where to report, anti-retaliation law, and why managers have a mandatory obligation to act on reports they receive, even informally.
Meet Stan. He means well but keeps things unprofessional — unsolicited backrubs, comments about a colleague's appearance, remarks about what's "sexy." This scenario shows clearly where the line is and why keeping it professional matters.
An upbeat Music Man-style musical number that drives home the core message: don't harass, don't make anyone feel inferior based on gender, sexual orientation, or transgender status. Memorable, fun, and genuinely hard to forget.
Stan is slowly figuring out that not-safe-for-work content really doesn't belong at work — on screens, in chats, or shared casually. This segment covers digital behavior and why context matters even for "just a joke."
What actually makes a workplace hostile in a legal sense? This lesson lays out the foundational definition — conduct that is severe or pervasive enough to create an abusive work environment — before a series of real-world scenarios brings it to life.
A coworker asks another out. The answer is no — and it's respected. This scenario models what healthy boundary-setting looks like and shows that asking someone out at work isn't inherently harassment, as long as the answer is accepted gracefully.
A female manager pressures a staff member to go out with her. This scenario deliberately flips gender expectations to reinforce that power dynamics — not gender — are what make this harassment. Anyone in authority can cross the line.
A single incident of explicit content in the workplace. This scenario explores the legal concept that even one occurrence can constitute harassment if it's severe enough — and why "it only happened once" isn't always a defense.
A vendor makes repeated low-level comments to an intern — nothing explosive, but relentless. This scenario tackles pervasive harassment: how a pattern of minor incidents can add up to a hostile environment, even when each incident seems small on its own.
Not everything awkward is harassment. This scenario shows an uncomfortable compliment that is neither severe nor pervasive — just clumsy. Helping employees calibrate what actually crosses the line is as important as showing what does.
After the scenarios, this lesson consolidates the two-part test: conduct must be both severe or pervasive, and meet both a subjective standard (the person experiencing it found it hostile) and an objective standard (a reasonable person would too).
Stan is curious — which is fine. But his questions about a coworker's background and identity cross into territory that feels othering and unprofessional. This segment covers cultural sensitivity: curiosity is human, but there's a line. Employees also learn they can set reasonable limits on what they share.
Everyone has a role in making the workplace better. This lesson covers the four bystander strategies — Direct, Delay, Distract, and Delegate — and gives employees practical, low-risk ways to respond when they see something that doesn't seem right.
Stan's confident — and that's actually great. This final Stan segment draws the distinction between self-assurance and unwelcome flirting or pursuit. Confidence is an asset. Ignoring signals that your attention isn't wanted is not.
A dedicated module covering California's Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA), SB 1343 training requirements, and state-specific protections that go beyond federal law. Recommended for any organization with California employees.
Course complete. Stan improved — and so did you. This closing reinforces the key takeaways, reminds employees where to go if they need support or want to report something, and sends everyone off feeling equipped, not lectured.
